## Field Progress

- AI in solar-cell research is shifting from isolated prediction tasks to integrated workflows that couple forecasting, optimization, and uncertainty quantification.
- Physics-informed ML is becoming the preferred route for PV applications because purely data-driven models struggle with weather variability, device heterogeneity, and operating-regime changes.
- Perovskite characterization is becoming more local and more dynamic, with new methods directly resolving defects, surface potentials, excitons, ions, and carrier motion.
- Materials AI is becoming more collaborative and more constrained, using symbolic rules, curated datasets, and human feedback to improve synthesis success rates.
- Inline optical and hyperspectral inspection methods suggest that manufacturing data streams are becoming richer and better suited for AI-based quality monitoring.
- Theoretical advances in excited-state and atomistic modeling are extending the scale and realism of simulations needed to connect materials structure to device behavior.

## Open Questions

- How can PV forecasting models be made reliably robust across unseen climates, plant configurations, and weather-model error regimes?
- What is the best way to combine symbolic chemistry reasoning with generative LLMs so that inverse-design suggestions remain synthesizable and experimentally useful?
- Can defect-fingerprinting and carrier-dynamics imaging be standardized into practical workflows for rapid perovskite screening and passivation design?
- How should multimodal data from spectroscopy, microscopy, and inline metrology be fused with MLIPs to predict device degradation and manufacturing yield?
- What level of human oversight is needed for LLM-driven synthesis planning to remain safe, reproducible, and scientifically productive?
- Can foundation interatomic potentials and multitask electrostatic models generalize to the strongly correlated and defect-rich materials most relevant to next-generation photovoltaics?

## Outlook

The July 2026 literature suggests a clear trajectory: AI is becoming more valuable in solar-cell research when it is tightly coupled to physics, uncertainty awareness, and experimental feedback. The near-term opportunity is not fully autonomous discovery, but robust scientific copilots that can help design syntheses, interpret measurements, forecast PV output, and monitor manufacturing in real time. The main challenge ahead is integration: connecting foundation models, symbolic constraints, and high-fidelity device physics into workflows that are both predictive and experimentally trustworthy.
